Bio-inspired Floating Foundations for Offshore Wind Turbines

Abstract

With global climate change necessitating a shift toward sustainable energy, deep-water wind energy remains a largely untapped resource due to higher wind speeds and greater energy potential. However, transitioning to deeper waters requires wind turbines to be mounted on floating foundations that can withstand harsh oceanic conditions. As part of this study, we explored bio-inspired designs—including tree fractals, flower fractals, and honeycomb lattice structures—to address the stability, manufacturability, and fatigue durability challenges of existing floating platforms. Through an iterative design-print-test cycle, six unique foundations were developed and evaluated in a controlled water environment with simulated wind currents. The research demonstrates that structures mimicking biological efficiency, such as the "Mixed" and "Extreme Lotus" designs, provide superior resistance to turbulence and improved structural redundancy. This work establishes a baseline for next-generation offshore infrastructure that leverages the natural resilience found in biological systems.

Objective

  • To design and validate bio-inspired floating offshore wind turbine (FOWT) foundations that improve stability and fatigue durability compared to traditional semi-submersible or spar-buoy platforms.
  • To evaluate the hydrodynamic and structural performance of different biological geometries using a scaled experimental model.
  • To identify design features that enhance manufacturability and operational life in deep-water environments

Methodology

Iterative Design Process:

  • Developed 3D models using Tinkercad based on three primary biological inspirations: tree fractals (branching structures), flower fractals (lotus patterns), and honeycomb lattices.
  • Optimized designs for 3D printing, ensuring structural integrity while minimizing material use.

Experimental Testing Environment:

  • Conducted stability tests in a controlled water tub, using a box fan to simulate various wind speeds and ocean currents.
  • Performed static load testing using a hydraulic press to assess the fatigue resilience and structural redundancy of the designs.

Data Acquisition and Analysis:

  • Integrated a 9-axis Inertial Measurement Unit (IMU) with the model to record real-time accelerometer and gyroscope data during wind testing.
  • Analyzed stability through metrics such as the standard deviation from the mean position and the decay rate of oscillations.
  • Utilized radar graphs to compare performance across multiple parameters, including load capacity, damping, and manufacturing ease.

Results and Discussion

  • Stability Performance: The "Mixed" design exhibited the lowest standard deviation, indicating the highest stability under turbulent wind conditions.
  • Dynamic Response: The "Lotus" designs showed a high decay rate, meaning the structure returned to equilibrium quickly after being disturbed by a gust.
  • Structural Integrity: Latticed structures like the "Honeycomb" offered high manufacturability but required careful optimization to handle the concentrated loads of the turbine.
  • Redundancy: Fractal designs (Tree and Flower) demonstrated high redundancy, meaning the failure of a single "branch" or "petal" did not lead to the immediate catastrophic failure of the entire foundation.

Conclusion

  • Bio-inspired geometries provide a viable and efficient alternative to traditional offshore engineering designs.
  • Hybrid structures that combine different biological patterns offer the best balance of stability, damping, and load capacity.
  • Future research will involve COMSOL Multiphysics simulations and testing with larger-scale, more durable materials like high-performance concrete.
